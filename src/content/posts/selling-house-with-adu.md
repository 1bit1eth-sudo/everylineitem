This post is about the transaction. **It is not legal advice.** Real estate law is fact-specific; talk to a California real estate attorney before you list.

An ADU changes the math of a sale in four places: what you must **disclose**, how the **appraiser** treats the unit, how you **market** the property, and **who shows up** to buy it. Get any one wrong and the deal dies in escrow instead of at the kitchen table.

## The short answer

| Change | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **Disclosure** | California's TDS requires disclosing known material facts — including unpermitted work (Civil Code §1102.6) |
| **Permitted ADU** | Appraises with contributory value; markets as a legal unit with income history |
| **Unpermitted ADU** | Often appraises as bonus space or nothing; financed buyers may walk; disclose and price accordingly |
| **Two-ADU lot (AB 956)** | Effective Jan 1, 2027 — market the *eligibility*, not a built second unit |
| **Buyer pool** | Widens toward investors, multigenerational families, house-hackers — but financed buyers need the paperwork clean |
| **Property tax** | Your sale reassesses the whole property at market value for the buyer (Prop 13 change of ownership) |

## 1. Disclosure: the Transfer Disclosure Statement

California sellers of one-to-four unit residential property must deliver a **Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)** — Civil Code §§1102–1102.17, with the specific questionnaire at §1102.6 (the C.A.R. Form TDS). The seller must disclose, in honesty and good faith, known conditions that could affect the property's value or desirability. The form specifically asks about:

- **Room additions, structural modifications, or other alterations or repairs made without necessary permits**
- **The same made not in compliance with building codes**

An unpermitted ADU — or an ADU whose permits were never finaled — is exactly what those questions are fishing for. Three things sellers get wrong:

1. **"As-is" doesn't erase disclosure.** California transactions are "as disclosed," not "as-is" — a buyer cannot waive the statutory TDS, and an as-is clause doesn't excuse hiding known permit problems.
2. **Disclosure isn't limited to the checkboxes.** The statute reaches any known material fact, even if no line on the form names it.
3. **Disclosure doesn't obligate you to fix it.** Disclosing an unpermitted ADU doesn't mean you must permit it before closing — it means the buyer knows, and the price reflects it.

Your listing agent has an independent duty too: a visual inspection of the property and disclosure of observable conditions affecting value (Civil Code §2079). If the ADU is visibly a conversion and the permits are murky, expect the agent's inspection to flag it.

## 2. Permitted vs. unpermitted: the value gap

This is where our [appraisal guide](/posts/adu-appraised-value/) meets the transaction. The same unit, two different paper trails, two different deals:

| | Permitted, finaled ADU | Unpermitted / non-finaled ADU |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Appraiser** | Contributory value via paired sales; separate line in the grid per Fannie Mae B4-1.3-05 | Little or no contributory value; treated as ancillary space at best |
| **Lender** | Standard underwriting | May not count the unit; some programs balk at unpermitted living space |
| **Marketing** | "Legal 2-bed ADU, rented at $X/mo" | "Bonus space" — never "bedroom" or "unit" in the listing |
| **Buyer pool** | Full pool, including financed buyers | Cash and risk-tolerant buyers; financed buyers may need the appraisal to work without the ADU |
| **Escrow risk** | Low on the ADU itself | Appraisal gap, lender conditions, buyer cold feet |

**Practical options for the unpermitted ADU, in rough order of effort:**

- **Retroactive permitting before listing.** Slow and sometimes expensive — open permits, bring work up to current code — but it converts the unit from liability to asset. Get a contractor's read on feasibility before committing.
- **Disclose and price as bonus space.** The honest discount path: the market treats it as flexible square footage, not a rentable dwelling.
- **Sell to an investor buyer.** Investors underwrite the permit risk themselves — at a discount that reflects it.

What you must not do: market an unpermitted unit as a legal ADU, quote its "rental income" as established fact in the listing, or hope nobody checks the permit file. Buyers' agents check the permit file.

## 3. The AB 956 angle: selling a two-ADU lot

As of this post's publication (December 4, 2026), **AB 956 is signed but not yet effective.** The law — Chapter 791, Statutes of 2026, amending Government Code §66323 — requires cities to ministerially approve up to **two detached, new-construction ADUs** per single-family lot starting **January 1, 2027** (each up to 800 sq ft per city cap). Our full explainer: [/posts/3-adu-laws-january-2027/](/posts/3-adu-laws-january-2027/).

For a seller listing in December 2026, that timing creates a specific marketing line — and a specific trap:

- **The line:** your lot will be eligible for two detached ADUs in under a month. For investor buyers and house-hackers, that's a real option value: two rental units on a single-family lot, ministerially approved, no discretionary review.
- **The trap:** eligibility is not entitlement. Nothing has been approved, no permits exist, and the second unit is still hypothetical. Marketing copy that implies a two-unit property *exists* is misrepresentation. Say "eligible for two detached ADUs under AB 956 effective Jan 2027" — precise, dated, and true.
- **The tradeoff to disclose:** building two detached ADUs means the city is no longer required to approve a JADU — and SB 1117's impact-fee break applies only to lots with up to two ADUs. A sophisticated buyer will ask; have the answer ready.

If you've already built one detached ADU and the lot can take a second, you're selling the most AB 956-ready product on the market: one income stream in hand, one ministerial approval away.

## 4. Who buys a house with an ADU

The buyer pool for ADU properties is wider than for a plain single-family home — but it's segmented, and each segment underwrites differently:

- **Investors and house-hackers.** They run the rent math from our [payback post](/posts/adu-rent-payback-roi-california/) and bid on yield. Clean permits and a rent history are worth more to them than granite counters.
- **Multigenerational families.** Parents, adult children, caregivers — they buy housing utility, not ROI. The ADU's layout (accessibility, privacy, separate entrance) matters more than its rent comp.
- **First-time buyers stretching.** An ADU's rental income can make the monthly payment workable — but whether a lender counts that income toward qualification is the lender's call under their program rules, not the listing agent's promise. Never advertise "the ADU pays your mortgage" as a financing fact.
- **Flippers (post-AB 956).** Two-ADU eligibility adds a development angle to single-family lots. Expect sharper offers and faster due diligence from this segment.

**Marketing that works:** lead with the paperwork, not the lifestyle. "Permitted 2024, finaled, separately metered, currently leased at $X/mo with 12-month history" beats "charming guest house!" in every segment that can actually close.

## 5. The pre-listing checklist

Two to four weeks before listing, assemble what the appraiser, the lender, and the buyer's agent will all ask for:

1. **Permit file** — building permit, finaled inspections, certificate of occupancy. Pull it from the city yourself; don't discover gaps during escrow.
2. **ADU specs sheet** — square footage, bed/bath count, year built, separate metering, parking.
3. **Rent history** — leases, rent rolls, or (if family-occupied) a rent comp set built with our rent-estimation method (publishing Dec 8 in this series).
4. **Code enforcement clearance** — no open violations on the parcel. One open case can stall a lender.
5. **HOA position in writing** — if applicable. State law limits HOA bans on ADUs, but CC&Rs still generate questions; have the documents ready.
6. **Prop 13 briefing for the buyer** — the buyer's taxes will be based on the purchase price (change of ownership), not your protected base-year value. No surprises at the closing table.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I finish permitting my ADU before selling?**

If the work is close to code and the city offers a path, usually yes — the value recovered as a legal unit typically exceeds the permit cost. If the conversion is far from code (setbacks, fire separation, ceiling height), get a contractor's feasibility read first; sometimes the honest move is disclosing and discounting.

**Can the buyer's appraiser use my ADU's rental income?**

They can consider it under the income approach if the unit is legal and rentable — but the sales comparison approach still anchors the opinion, and thin ADU comp data limits everyone equally. See our appraisal guide.

**Does a JADU affect a sale differently than a detached ADU?**

Yes, at the margins: a JADU shares the house, so it reads as flexible living space rather than a separate dwelling — simpler appraisal, smaller contributory value, fewer lender questions. Under AB 956's tradeoff, a lot with two detached ADUs may lose the by-right JADU — worth knowing if your marketing leans on "three units possible."

**I'm selling in December 2026. Can I advertise the second ADU under AB 956?**

Advertise the *eligibility*, precisely dated: "Lot eligible for a second detached ADU under AB 956, effective January 1, 2027." Do not imply it exists or is approved.
