> **DRAFT — not for publication until reviewed.**

An 800 sq ft detached ADU in El Monte costs at least **$306,087, or about $383 per square foot**, on verified figures alone — and the number that carries the post is a single prorated stack. Anyone searching for El Monte ADU fees will find a city that publishes its impact fees with unusual clarity: every component of the single-family stack is itemized to the cent, and the technology surcharge is spelled out as a percentage rather than buried. What they will not find, in the same year's documents, is a building permit table they can price an ADU from, or a school-fee pair they can add with confidence. This post counts the stack, prorates it exactly as the state rule requires, and leaves the rest blank on purpose.

El Monte is the batch's lesson in why a floor can be both precise and incomplete at the same time. The $4,087 impact fee is arithmetically exact given the stated primary-home assumption, and it moves by a known amount when that assumption changes. The permit bill, by contrast, cannot be priced at all from current documents, so the gap between this floor and a real budget is at least one whole fee category wide — and the Quimby question alone could be larger than the counted fee several times over.

The soft-costs phase is $14,000 of design from the series baseline plus the $4,087 prorated impact fee. Everything else that usually lives in that phase — the building permit, plan check, school fees, and any separate-connection charge — is excluded, not estimated. A floor that quietly absorbed guesses would stop being a floor.

One scope note, because readers often ask for the companion figure: this post does not publish a verified total for a new 2,000 sq ft single-family home in El Monte. The fee schedule prices permits and impact fees but never prices construction, so no honest total can be derived from it. The 2,000 sq ft figure appears here only as the denominator that makes the example legible — an 800 sq ft ADU is 40% of a 2,000 sq ft primary home, which is the proportion the state's impact-fee rule uses once the 750 sq ft cliff is crossed.

Two details in that table deserve emphasis. First, the proration denominator is doing real work: the same stack is $4,087 beside a 2,000 sq ft primary home, $5,109 beside a 1,600 sq ft one, and $6,812 beside a 1,200 sq ft one. Any El Monte quote that states an impact fee without stating the primary home's size is missing the variable that determines it. Second, the Quimby exclusion is the largest single judgment in this post, and it cuts against the floor: if the city does charge Quimby on ADUs, even prorated, this floor is low by thousands. That risk is stated here rather than priced, because pricing it either way would require an assumption the schedule does not support.

> **The 750 sq ft rule, in plain terms.** California's ADU fee statute, Government Code section 65852.2(f)(3)(A), bars any impact fee on an ADU smaller than 750 square feet, and requires that an ADU of 750 square feet or more be charged only in proportion to the primary dwelling's square footage. Our 800 sq ft example therefore crosses the cliff in principle, and the $4,087 above is the prorated result. From January 1, 2027, SB 1117 (Government Code section 66311.5) changes the treatment for lots with two or fewer ADUs: only the area in excess of 750 square feet is charged, so an 800 sq ft unit beside a 2,000 sq ft primary home would be charged on 50 sq ft — about $255 on this stack. That future rule is not in this post's 2026 total. Connection and capacity charges are a separate category from impact fees, which is why sewer and water lines stay in their own row. For the full explanation of how the cliff works, see [The 750 sq ft cliff](/posts/750-sq-ft-cliff-explained/).

## Three things that can still move the number

**1. Quimby, in either direction.** The schedule lists Quimby beside the stack without an ADU sentence, and the verified record excludes it pending confirmation. A full-rate charge adds $15,053; a prorated charge adds $6,021; an exemption adds nothing. No other open question in this post is that large, and it is the first question to ask the city.

**2. The building permit and plan check.** The FY 2025-26 building schedule was not in the retrieved text, and the FY 2024-25 edition cannot price a 2026 permit without a current valuation basis. When the city confirms the current table and valuation rate, the permit and plan check join the floor by addition. Until then, treat any El Monte permit quote that does not cite a schedule edition as unverified — including the third-party survey figure of $8,500 noted in the FAQ, which is not the city's own schedule.

**3. The school fee and the primary-home denominator.** The school fee needs the parcel's district pair, confirmed from district documents, before it can be summed at all; the statewide cap is not a substitute, as [ADU school fees, explained](/posts/adu-school-fees-explained/) shows for split districts generally. Separately, the counted $4,087 assumes a 2,000 sq ft primary home — re-price it at the actual primary size before budgeting, using the stack table above, because the difference between a 1,200 sq ft and a 2,000 sq ft primary home is $2,725 on this line alone.

The series baseline is shared, so the comparison isolates local fees rather than construction costs. El Monte sits below the LA County range, but the comparison is deliberately uneven: El Monte's figure is missing its permit bill, its plan check, and its school fee, while the LA County figures include theirs. Once those blanks fill in, expect the gap to narrow or reverse — especially if Quimby applies. What El Monte genuinely leads on is transparency of the one fee it does publish: a stack itemized to the cent beats a single lump sum that cannot be checked.

How to use this floor in practice: treat $306,087 as the number below which the project should not be budgeted, re-price the impact fee at the actual primary-home size, and carry separate, explicit allowances for Quimby, the permit and plan check, the school fee, and any separate-connection charge. When the city answers those questions, the floor converts into a budget by addition, not by revision. That is the point of keeping guesses out of the total in the first place.
