> **Published:** 7 Nov 2026 · **Last verified:** 7 Nov 2026 · **Next review:** Jan 2027

Every ADU applicant knows the loop. You submit plans. Weeks later, a correction list arrives. You fix everything on it, resubmit, and wait again — and the second list contains new items the first list never mentioned. Then a third round. Then a fourth. Each round costs weeks, and weeks on a $320,000 build cost real money in design carry, financing, and construction inflation.

On September 29, 2026, Governor Newsom signed **AB 1621** (Wilson; Chapter 780, Statutes of 2026). Effective **January 1, 2027**, it puts a number on the loop: **two rounds**. Not two rounds as a guideline — two rounds as a statutory cap, with a written-findings escape hatch the agency has to justify on the record. It is not an ADU bill. It amends the postentitlement permit rules that govern every housing building permit in the state, and an ADU permit is one of them.

Here is what it does, in the order it hits your permit.

## 1. The two-round cap — Gov. Code §65913.3

**What:** A local or state agency "may not require or request more than two plan check and specification reviews in connection with a building permit application" for a housing development project — unless the requirement or request is accompanied by written findings, based on substantial evidence in the record, that the additional review is necessary to address a specific, adverse impact on public health or safety.

**The escape hatch is narrow on purpose.** "We found more things" is not a finding. The agency has to put in writing, on the record, that the extra review addresses a specific, adverse impact on public health or safety — and that finding has to be supported by substantial evidence. A third round because the reviewer was thorough is not that. A third round because the plans genuinely endanger occupants might be.

**Scope:** the cap applies regardless of project size — one ADU or one hundred units. It applies to local agencies and, following AB 301 (Chapter 488, Statutes of 2025), to state agencies too. And it applies to all cities, including charter cities.

## 2. The full-comments rule — the mechanism that makes the cap bite

A two-round cap would mean nothing if the agency could hold comments back for round two. The bill's review procedure closes that gap: on each review, the agency must complete its review and either return the approved permit application or return **a full set of comments in writing, with a comprehensive request for revisions** — and notify the applicant electronically of its determination.

The deadline to act is **30 business days** for projects of 25 units or fewer (45 for 26 or more) — the deadlines AB 1621 sets for postentitlement permits, building on the framework AB 2234 (Chapter 651, Statutes of 2022) established. An ADU, at one unit, is in the 30-day bucket.

The practical effect: the city has to show you everything it wants fixed, the first time. A correction that appears for the first time in round two is, by the bill's design, a correction the city should have caught in round one — and after round two, the city cannot demand a third round to chase it without the written findings above.

## 3. Denial after two rounds — and who gets to keep going

**What:** After two plan check and specification reviews, the agency **may deny** an application that does not comply with the applicable permit standards. And the applicant **may request additional submittals** of applications that are not compliant with permit standards.

This is the part people misread. The cap is not a guarantee of approval. If your plans still do not comply after two rounds, the city can deny the permit. What changes is who controls the next step: the applicant can ask for additional submittals. The city cannot force a third round on its own initiative; the applicant can choose to keep submitting. That asymmetry matters — it stops the loop from being something done *to* you, and turns it into something you opt into, with your eyes open, after a denial you can appeal.

## 4. Tolling, narrowed — and a three-day notice

**What:** The statutory review deadlines are tolled — paused — **only** where federal or state law requires review by another independent public agency before the permitting agency may act. And the agency must notify the applicant **within three business days** after tolling begins, and again within three business days after the deadline resumes.

Under the prior practice, a referral to an outside entity could pause the clock with little visibility. Now the pause needs a legal basis (another agency's review is *required by law*, not merely convenient), and you find out about it within three business days — twice. For an ADU, the outside reviewer is usually the school district, a sewer district, or a fire district; the rule does not remove those reviews, it makes their effect on your timeline visible.

## 5. No rewriting the approved plan at inspection

**What:** A local or state agency may not request or require any action — or inaction — as a result of a building inspection that deviates from a previously approved plan or similar approval, unless it makes written findings, supported by substantial evidence, that **either** a reasonable person could not interpret the approved plan as compliant with the applicable building permit standards, **or** the deviation is necessary to address a specific, adverse impact on public health or safety.

This is the second loop AB 1621 closes. The first is plan check; the second is the inspector in the field asking for something the approved plans do not show. From 2027, "the inspector wants it different" is not a reason. The inspector's request has to clear the same written-findings bar — and the agency has to show its work on the record.

## 6. Faster appeals — and a writ if the city stalls

**What:** If an applicant appeals an incomplete determination, a noncompliance determination, or a denial, the agency's final written determination is due in **30 business days** for projects of 25 units or fewer (down from 60), and **45 business days** for projects of 26 units or more (down from 90).

And if the appeal is denied, or not decided within the deadline, or the required appeal process is not provided at all, the applicant **may seek a writ of mandate** to compel approval. A violation of the specified postentitlement requirements also counts as a **disapproval under the Housing Accountability Act** for a local agency — opening HAA remedies — while a state agency violation results in the permit being deemed approved and the related reviews deemed complete.

The bill passed with almost no opposition in the Legislature — 40–0 in the Senate, 76–0 on Assembly concurrence — but the California State Association of Counties opposed it and asked the Governor for a veto. The Governor signed it anyway.

## What it means for an ADU, specifically

AB 1621 is a general housing-permit law, not an ADU law — so its ADU effect is specific, and worth stating precisely.

| Your situation | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| ADU building permit submitted on or after Jan 1, 2027 | **Two-round cap applies** — the city gets two plan check and specification reviews, then must approve, deny, or justify a further round in writing |
| ADU permit already in plan check in 2026 | **Old rules apply** — the bill is not retroactive; rounds already consumed stay consumed |
| Planning / ADU ministerial review at the planning counter | **Cap does not apply** — the two-round limit covers building permits only, not other postentitlement phase permits |
| Pre-approved ADU plan (e.g., Santa Ana $504 review vs $1,865 full review) | **Complementary** — a pre-approved plan starts with fewer corrections to argue about, and the cap limits how many rounds the argument can take |
| City misses the 30-day review deadline | **Private plan check is already available** — under AB 253 (2025), you may contract with a private professional provider if the city estimates more than 30 days or misses 30 days |
| Inspector asks for a change not on the approved plans | **Ask for the findings** — from Jan 1, the request needs written findings based on substantial evidence, or it is not a lawful request |

## What AB 1621 does not do

- **It does not cap plan-check fees.** Fullerton charges $1,064 for plan review (87% of its $1,223 building permit) plus a $245 energy plan check; Riverside charges $1,006 for permit + plan check; La Habra charges $13,798 for permit + plan check + surcharges. Those figures are set by city fee schedules, and AB 1621 does not touch them. Fewer rounds may mean fewer re-check fees where a city charges per round — but the bill does not set the price of a round.
- **It does not cover the planning review.** The ADU ministerial review, zoning compliance, and any discretionary planning permit are outside the two-round limit. If your delay is at the planning counter, this bill is not the lever.
- **It does not guarantee approval.** After two rounds, the city may deny a noncompliant application. The bill limits how long the city can keep you in review; it does not limit the city's ability to say no.
- **It does not change any fee.** Impact fees, school fees, water and sewer capacity charges, and construction taxes are untouched. The 750 sq ft cliff is SB 1117's job; the 500 sq ft school-fee line is SB 543's; capacity charges are SB 1036's.
- **It does not apply to permits already submitted.** Applications submitted before January 1, 2027 follow the prior rules. Timing a submission for January is a real choice — but weigh it against the cost of waiting, as with SB 1117.
- **It does not remove outside-agency review.** School districts, sewer districts, and fire districts still review ADU permits. The bill only narrows when those reviews can pause the city's clock, and requires notice when they do.

## The companion bill: SB 1014

AB 1621 was not the only 2026 bill aimed at the postentitlement stage. **SB 1014** (Grayson) gives applicants earlier information about required improvements: with a preliminary application, you can request a good-faith list of the improvements the agency may require and a good-faith cost estimate for the ones the agency would build, within 30 business days. And at the postentitlement stage, within 30 business days after your permit application is deemed complete, the permitting jurisdiction must list all required onsite and offsite improvements — and generally may not later require one it omitted, unless the improvement addresses a specific adverse health or safety impact supported by substantial evidence, is reasonably related to a requested change, or is required under CEQA. Together, the two bills attack the same problem from both ends: SB 1014 makes the city say what it wants early; AB 1621 stops the city from adding to the list late.

## The bottom line

Two rounds, one full list of comments, and a written reason for anything more. AB 1621 does not make plan check fast — 30 business days a round is still two months for two rounds, and a denial after round two is still a denial. What it does is make the process finite and visible: the city has to show its whole hand, on a clock, and explain itself in writing if it wants more. For an ADU applicant, whose permit is one unit in the 30-day bucket, that is the difference between a loop with no exit and a process with a decision at the end of it. January 1 is 55 days away.
