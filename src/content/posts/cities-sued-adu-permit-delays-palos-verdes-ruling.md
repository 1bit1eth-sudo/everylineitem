> This post is general information about a court decision and California statutes, not legal advice. Permit disputes turn on project-specific facts — talk to a California land-use attorney before acting on a deadline.

California law has long told cities to decide ADU permits fast and without discretionary second-guessing. Until now, it named no price for ignoring that instruction. On 23 October 2026, the Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District, Division Four, supplied one: a homeowner who says his city stalled his ADU permit for more than three years may sue the city for the damages the delay allegedly caused. The case is _Shi v. City of Palos Verdes Estates_, No. B350195, certified for publication. It does not hand any homeowner a check. It opens a door cities had argued was closed — and it makes the paper trail you keep during permitting matter more than before.

## What happened, as alleged

The homeowner applied to the City of Palos Verdes Estates in 2020 for a permit to build an ADU alongside the family home on his lot. He alleged the city did not decide the application ministerially, as state law requires. Instead, the city told him approval would require a conditional use permit, discretionary review, and sign-off from the Palos Verdes Homes Association Art Jury, and a city planning commissioner allegedly lobbied his neighbors against the project. The city did not approve the ADU permit until 2023 — more than three years after he applied.

The homeowner sued in October 2024 under Government Code section 815.6, part of the Government Claims Act, alleging the delay caused him loss of use of his property, increased construction costs, interest expense, design professional costs, and diminished property value. The trial court sustained the city's demurrer, reasoning that the ADU statutes exist to ease the statewide housing shortage, not to protect individual homeowners from the costs of delay. The homeowner appealed, and the Court of Appeal reversed.

One procedural note matters for everything that follows. A demurrer tests whether a complaint states a claim, taking its allegations as true. The court therefore decided a legal question — whether the alleged facts, if proved, can support a damages claim — not whether the city actually delayed anything. The case returns to the trial court, where the homeowner must still prove breach and causation.

## The two statutes that decide the case

| Statute          | What it says                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Why it matters here                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Gov. Code §66317 | An ADU permit application "shall be considered and approved ministerially without discretionary review or a hearing." When an existing dwelling is on the lot, the agency must approve or deny a completed application within 60 days; if it does neither, the application is deemed approved. No local ordinance, policy, or regulation inconsistent with the ADU article may be the basis for delay or denial. | This is the mandatory duty the homeowner says the city breached. When the homeowner applied in 2020, the same requirements sat in former §65852.2; the Legislature repealed and renumbered that section as §66317 in 2024, and the parties agreed the requirements are substantially the same.                                                             |
| Gov. Code §815.6 | "Where a public entity is under a mandatory duty imposed by an enactment that is designed to protect against the risk of a particular kind of injury, the public entity is liable for an injury of that kind proximately caused by its failure to discharge the duty unless the public entity establishes that it exercised reasonable diligence to discharge the duty."                                         | This is the damages vehicle. It has three elements: a mandatory duty imposed by an enactment, an enactment designed to protect against the particular kind of injury suffered, and proximate causation. The city did not dispute the first or third elements, so the court assumed them without deciding and ruled only on the second. |

The city's argument was narrow, and the court rejected it on its own terms. The city said the ADU statutes were written to alleviate the housing crisis, not to create a private right of action, and that they say nothing about damages. The court answered that the homeowner was not claiming the ADU statutes create a damages remedy. Section 815.6 creates the remedy; the ADU statute supplies the mandatory duty. And the duty's beneficiaries are not an abstraction: "Permit seekers are the direct and intended beneficiaries of the relevant duty." Allowing applicants to receive ministerial approval without discretionary requirements and burdensome local regulations, the court wrote, is not incidental to §66317 — it is the point of §66317.

The court also distinguished the city's lead authority, _Haggis v. City of Los Angeles_ (2000), where the Supreme Court held a recordation duty was meant to push existing landowners to do stabilization work, not to protect later buyers. Here the duty to decide promptly runs directly to the person waiting on the permit.

## What the ruling does not do

- **It awards nothing.** The only money the opinion orders is the homeowner's costs on appeal. No court has found the city liable or fixed any damages amount.
- **It does not decide causation.** The homeowner must still prove the delay, not something else, caused each category of loss he claims.
- **It does not erase the city's defense.** Section 815.6 excuses a public entity that "establishes that it exercised reasonable diligence to discharge the duty." That defense is still open on remand.
- **It does not change the 60-day rule itself.** The statute already required ministerial decisions in 60 days, with a deemed-approved backstop. What changed is the consequence a city faces for missing it.
- **It is one published opinion, not a statewide audit.** Commentators have suggested the §815.6 logic could reach other ministerial housing approvals beyond ADUs — but the holding itself concerns ADU permits under §66317.

## The checklist: what a delayed applicant should record

If your permit is drifting past the statutory clocks, your future options depend on records you create now. Keep everything dated and in writing:

1. **The completeness date.** The 60-day clock runs from a completed application, and the agency must tell you within 15 business days whether your application is complete. Get that determination in writing and calendar day 60 from the completed-application date.
2. **Every city demand, in writing.** If staff ask for a conditional use permit, a discretionary design review, a hearing, or a third party's sign-off, ask for the request and its legal basis in writing. Section 66317 forbids discretionary review and bars inconsistent local rules as a basis for delay — a written demand is the evidence that matters.
3. **Your responses, with dates.** Answer correction items promptly and keep dated copies of every resubmittal. Gaps you cause are gaps the city will attribute to you.
4. **The cost of waiting, as it accrues.** Log rate-lock expirations, loan interest, rent you cannot collect, redesign and re-permitting fees, and contractor price increases, each tied to a date and an invoice. The homeowner's alleged categories — loss of use, higher construction costs, interest, design fees, diminished value — are a useful template, not a guarantee any of them will be recoverable in your case.
5. **The deemed-approved question, in writing.** If 60 days pass with no decision, ask the agency in writing about the application's status under the deemed-approved provision — and get legal advice before treating any permit as approved on that basis.
6. **The claim clock.** Claims against a California city have their own short presentation deadlines, separate from any lawsuit deadline. Do not assume you can wait until construction ends to raise a delay claim; check the deadline early with counsel.

None of this converts a slow permit into a winning claim, and most delays still end the ordinary way: a follow-up call, a corrected plan set, a permit. The ruling's practical effect is leverage — a city weighing one more discretionary review now knows a court has recognized that delay itself can carry a damages price tag.
