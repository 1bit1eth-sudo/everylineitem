The 750 sq ft cliff gets the headlines, but a quieter trend is running underneath it: cities **waiving ADU fees outright**, no cliff required. Three California cities in our data have now zeroed out major fee categories for ADUs — each through a different legal mechanism, each with different durability.

## The three waivers

### Salinas: the full DIF waiver

Salinas is the first city in our series with a **complete development impact fee waiver for ADUs** — not a reduction, not a cliff, a waiver. The fee schedule simply doesn't apply the DIF to ADU permits. For an 800 sq ft unit, that's the entire impact-fee line — potentially $5,000–$10,000+ in peer cities — at $0.

Legal footing: fee schedule / council action. Durability: medium — a future council can reintroduce the fee, but there's no pending study or draft suggesting one.

### Oceanside: the ordinance says $0 (for now)

Oceanside's municipal ordinance waives **all** impact fees for ADUs — the strongest legal footing of the three, since amending an ordinance requires the full legislative process, not just a fee schedule update.

But there's a crack: a **2025 draft parks nexus study** (Economic & Planning Systems, August 22, 2025) led to a new parks fee schedule adopted November 19, 2025 (single-family $6.03/sq ft, effective January 20, 2026). Whether that schedule narrows the ADU waiver for units over 750 sq ft is not confirmed in the materials we reviewed. The ordinance says $0 today; the new schedule means the waiver deserves a fresh check. If you're building in Oceanside, the waiver is real but not permanent — verify the current ordinance before you budget, and don't assume the waiver is untouched.

### Oakland: the documented exemption

Oakland's ADU guidebook states ADUs are **exempt from impact fees** — the clearest single-document record of the three. Unlike Salinas (fee schedule) or Oceanside (ordinance with a new parks fee schedule whose effect on the waiver is unconfirmed), Oakland's exemption reads as settled policy: the city has considered the question and answered it.

## Why cities waive

The logic is straightforward: impact fees exist to make new development pay for the infrastructure load it creates. ADUs — particularly sub-750 sq ft units — create marginal load on infrastructure sized for the primary dwelling. Several cities have concluded the administrative cost of calculating, billing, and defending small ADU fees exceeds the revenue.

There's also a housing-policy motive. California's ADU laws (SB 13, AB 68, and their successors) steadily stripped cities' power to block ADUs; fee waivers are the carrot cities offer voluntarily, often tied to housing-element compliance pressure from HCD.

## The durability ranking

Not all $0s are equal:

1. **Oakland** — documented exemption in the city's ADU guidebook. Strongest signal of settled policy.
2. **Oceanside** — ordinance-level waiver, but a new parks fee schedule adopted in November 2025 leaves its effect on the waiver unconfirmed. Watch the council agenda.
3. **Salinas** — fee-schedule waiver, no pending threat identified. Solid until a council decides otherwise.

And the statewide backstop: **SB 1117** (effective January 1, 2027) reshapes the cliff everywhere — fees only on square footage above 750, for the first two ADUs. In high-fee cities, that's a bigger saving than any local waiver. In waiver cities, it's belt and suspenders.

## What this means for your build

1. **Check the waiver before you check the fee.** If your city waives, the entire impact-fee research step collapses to one confirmation.
2. **Confirm the legal instrument.** Ordinance > resolution > fee schedule, in amendment difficulty. Ask the planning counter which one your waiver lives in.
3. **Watch for studies.** A nexus study is the leading indicator that a waiver is about to narrow. Oceanside's 2025 parks study — adopted as a new fee schedule months later — is the template: if your city commissions one, budget as if the waiver has an expiration date.
4. **Waivers don't cover everything.** School district fees are set by school districts, not cities — no city waiver touches them. Plan-check, permit, and utility charges usually survive too.

## The bottom line

Three cities, three $0s, three different shelf lives. The waiver trend is real and growing — but a waived fee is a policy choice, not a physical law. Verify it exists on the day you pull the permit, not the day you read this post.
