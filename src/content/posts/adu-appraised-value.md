You spent $320,000 building a backyard ADU. How much of that does the appraiser give back when you refinance or sell? The short answer: **the appraiser doesn't care what it cost.** They care what the market pays for homes like yours — with and without the extra unit. This post explains the three approaches appraisers use, how Fannie Mae tells them to treat ADUs, and why the most honest number on this page is "it depends."

**This is not an appraisal.** It is an explanation of how appraisers work so you can ask better questions and prepare better evidence.

## The short answer

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Does an ADU usually add appraised value? | Yes — but as *contributory value* from market evidence, not dollar-for-dollar cost |
| Which approach matters most? | **Sales comparison** — paired sales of homes with ADUs vs. without, in your market |
| Will it add the full build cost? | Almost never; cost is not value |
| What kills contributory value? | Missing permits, no separate kitchen/bath, no comparable sales with ADUs nearby |
| Does appraised value change my taxes? | No — tax assessment is a separate system (see below) |

## The three approaches appraisers use

Licensed appraisers (in California, the Bureau of Real Estate Appraisers regulates them) develop value through up to three approaches, then reconcile into one opinion.

### 1. Sales comparison — the heavyweight

This is the primary approach for single-family homes with ADUs. The appraiser finds recent sales of **comparable properties** — ideally homes in your neighborhood, similar in size and quality, some with ADUs and some without. The difference in sale prices between the two groups is the **paired-sales evidence** for what the ADU contributes.

Two practical problems:

- **Comps with ADUs are scarce.** If your neighborhood has few ADU sales, the appraiser must search wider, go back further in time, or work with imperfect matches — all of which weaken the adjustment.
- **"Comparable" means ADU-to-ADU.** An appraiser cannot fairly compare your permitted 800 sq ft detached cottage to a listing that has a bootleg garage sleeping room. Kitchen, bath, permits, and finishes have to be in the same league.

This is why the answer is hyper-local. An 800 sq ft ADU in a rental-hungry LA neighborhood has plenty of paired-sales evidence; the same unit in a market where ADUs rarely sell can appraise at a fraction of its cost — not because it's worth less as housing, but because the evidence file is thin.

### 2. Income approach — for rented units

If the ADU is rented (or clearly rentable), the appraiser can estimate value from income: the unit's net operating income capitalized into a value. The mechanics are simple — value equals income divided by a capitalization rate — but every input is market-specific:

- **Rent** comes from comparable ADU rents, not apartment listings (see our rent-estimation guide for how to build that comp set).
- **The cap rate** reflects what investors in your market accept for small residential income property. No single published California ADU cap rate exists that we can cite; appraisers derive it from sales of comparable income properties.
- **Vacancy, management, and expenses** get deducted before capitalization.

The income approach is strongest where the sales comparison approach is weakest — markets with thin ADU sales but thick rental evidence — and vice versa. Appraisers reconcile the two rather than picking a favorite.

### 3. Cost approach — the cross-check

Replacement cost of the improvements, minus depreciation, plus land value. Your $320,000 construction invoice lives here — and this approach is usually the **weakest** of the three for ADUs, because:

- New construction hasn't depreciated, so the cost approach tends to *overstate* value relative to what buyers pay.
- Appraisers and lenders know cost ≠ market value. A $320,000 unit in a market where buyers only pay a $150,000 premium for ADU properties is, for appraisal purposes, worth the market premium.

**Rule of thumb for owners:** cost is what you spent; value is what the market's sales evidence says. The appraiser reports the latter.

## What Fannie Mae requires appraisers to do with ADUs

Most purchase and refinance appraisals follow Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, which has a dedicated ADU section ([B4-1.3-05, "Improvements Section of the Appraisal Report"](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-1.3-05/improvements-section-appraisal-report)). The key requirements:

- **Separate line in the grid.** The ADU's living area must **not** be included in the primary dwelling's gross living area. It is reported and adjusted on its own line — unless the ADU is inside the primary dwelling with interior access and above grade.
- **Describe and analyze.** The report must describe the ADU and include an **analysis of any effect it has on the value or marketability** of the property. An aged settled sale can serve as a comparable; active listings or pending sales can supplement the marketability case.
- **Non-compliant structures get downgraded.** If a standalone structure doesn't meet ADU minimum requirements (living, sleeping, cooking, and bathroom facilities on the same parcel), it's treated as an ordinary ancillary structure — adjusted only on its contributory value, not as a dwelling unit.

The takeaway: the guide doesn't hand the ADU a value. It hands the appraiser a procedure for *finding* one from evidence.

## So what does a $320,000 build appraise at?

Honestly: **we won't give you a number, because no citable one exists.** Nobody publishes a statewide average ADU contributory value for California with a methodology we'd stand behind. Anyone quoting "ADUs add 25%" or "you'll get $200,000 back" is doing marketing, not math.

What we can say, from the methodology:

| Situation | Likely appraisal treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Permitted detached ADU, own kitchen/bath, neighborhood has ADU comps | Full paired-sales contributory value — the best case |
| Permitted ADU, but no nearby ADU comps | Weaker sales evidence; income approach carries more weight |
| Unpermitted conversion (no finaled permits) | Often little or no contributory value; appraiser may treat it as ancillary space only |
| JADU inside the house | Contributory value as interior living area, not as a separate dwelling |
| Two-ADU lot (post-AB 956, Jan 2027) | No paired-sales history yet — appraisers will be working from thin evidence for years |

The recurring pattern: **permits and evidence are the two currencies.** Permits decide whether the unit can be valued as a dwelling at all; nearby ADU sales decide how much.

## Before the appraiser visits: your evidence file

Appraisers develop their own comps, but owners who hand over a clean file get cleaner results. Assemble:

1. **Permit records** — the building permit, all finaled inspections, certificate of occupancy if your city issues one. This is the single highest-leverage item.
2. **Plans and specs** — square footage, bedroom/bath count, finishes, separate utility metering.
3. **Lease or rent history** — for the income approach; even a signed lease at market rent is evidence.
4. **Your own comp research** — recent neighborhood sales of homes with ADUs. The appraiser will verify independently, but pointing at the right sales saves everyone time.
5. **Photos** — interior and exterior, current condition. Condition ratings affect the adjustment.

What *doesn't* help: your construction contract total (see above — cost is not value), Zillow estimates, or what a neighbor "thinks" their unit added.

## Appraised value ≠ assessed value ≠ listing price

Three different numbers, three different systems:

| Number | Who sets it | What it's for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Appraised value** | Licensed appraiser | Lender decisions, refinances, sales |
| **Assessed value** | County assessor | Property tax — ADU triggers reassessment of the *new construction only* under Prop 13 ([our tax guide](/posts/will-an-adu-raise-my-property-tax-california/)) |
| **Listing price** | You and your agent | Marketing — aspirational until a buyer and an appraiser agree |

A refinance appraisal can come in below your build cost *and* your county can still add the ADU's value to your tax roll. Both can be correct at once — they're answering different questions.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I get an appraisal before building an ADU?**

A pre-construction appraisal can't value what doesn't exist yet, but a local appraiser can tell you what ADU properties are selling for in your neighborhood — the paired-sales evidence that will eventually decide your unit's contributory value. That's cheap research before a $300,000 commitment.

**Does a rented ADU appraise higher than an empty one?**

Potentially — a signed lease at market rent gives the income approach real inputs instead of estimates. But the sales comparison approach still anchors the final opinion, and owner-occupied vs. rented doesn't change the paired-sales math.

**Can I appeal a low ADU appraisal?**

You can request a **reconsideration of value** through your lender, supplying better comps or correcting factual errors (wrong square footage, missed permits, misclassified ADU). It works when the appraiser missed evidence — not when you simply dislike the market.

**Does AB 956 (two detached ADUs per lot, effective Jan 1, 2027) change appraisals?**

Not immediately. Appraisers need *sales* of two-ADU properties to value the second unit's contribution, and those sales won't exist in volume until the law has been in effect for a while. Early two-ADU properties will appraise on thin evidence. Our three-laws explainer covers the legislation: [/posts/3-adu-laws-january-2027/](/posts/3-adu-laws-january-2027/).
