Your HOA's CC&Rs say "no second dwelling units." Your city issued the ADU permit anyway. Who wins?

**The city permit wins.** Since January 1, 2020, California law has held that an HOA covenant barring ADUs is not a rule — it's a suggestion the law refuses to enforce. Here's exactly where the line is.

## The void-ban rule: Civil Code 4751 (AB 670)

AB 670, effective January 1, 2020, added Civil Code **4751**: any covenant, restriction, or condition in a **planned development** that *effectively prohibits or unreasonably restricts* the construction or use of an ADU or JADU on a single-family lot is **void and unenforceable**.

That covers the three things HOAs historically did:

1. **Outright bans** — "no second units" language in the CC&Rs. Void.
2. **Unreasonable restrictions** — setbacks, height limits, or design rules that go beyond what state ADU law allows. Void to the extent they're unreasonable.
3. **Backdoor bans** — approval processes designed so no ADU could ever survive them. Void.

The statute's test for what survives is explicit: a "reasonable restriction" is only enforceable if it does **not** unreasonably increase the cost to construct, effectively prohibit, or unreasonably extinguish the ability to build the ADU. Cost is part of the test — a design rule that adds $40,000 to your build is not "reasonable" just because it mentions architecture.

## The extension: Civil Code 714.3 (2022)

Section 4751 covers common interest developments. Civil Code **714.3**, effective January 1, 2022, extended similar protection to **non-CID planned developments** — subdivisions with HOAs but without the common-area ownership structure of a CID. If your neighborhood has an HOA but no shared common area, you're still shielded.

**The real exception: condominiums.** Condominium associations **can** still prohibit ADUs. The ADU protections attach to single-family lots; a condo regime — where the "lot" is a unit within a shared building — is a different legal animal. If you own a condo, check with the association before spending a dollar on plans.

## What your HOA can still do

The law didn't strip HOAs of all authority — it confined them to *reasonable* rules. What survives:

- **Reasonable design guidelines.** Color matching the primary residence, architectural style consistency, landscaping standards. "Make it look like it belongs" is enforceable; "make it cost $50,000 more to look like it belongs" is not.
- **Height and massing within state limits.** The HOA can apply state ADU height rules (generally 16–25 feet depending on configuration), but it can't invent a stricter height cap that makes the ADU unbuildable.
- **Architectural review with timelines.** The HOA can run a design review process — but it must have stated timelines and criteria. An open-ended review with no decision deadline is exactly the kind of backdoor ban the statute voids.

## What your HOA can't do

- **Ban ADUs outright.** The classic CC&R prohibition is void and unenforceable. You don't need the HOA's permission to build what the law says it can't forbid.
- **Impose owner-occupancy on ADUs.** State law reserves owner-occupancy strings for JADUs ([see our owner-occupancy guide](/posts/adu-owner-occupancy-rules/)) — an HOA can't add one to a standard ADU.
- **Deny long-term rentals.** HOAs cannot prohibit renting the ADU for terms of 30 days or longer. (The 30-day floor is state law — short-term rentals are already off the table. The HOA can't push the restriction further.)
- **Require separate, stricter plans.** There is one plan set. The drawings you submit to the HOA's architectural committee and the drawings you submit to the city's building department are the same set — an HOA can't demand a different, more expensive design for its own review.

## The practical playbook

1. **Read your CC&Rs before you need them.** Know what the documents actually say — half of all HOA "ADU bans" are unenforced dead language from the 1990s that a written citation to 4751 resolves.
2. **Submit early, in writing.** Get your plans in front of the architectural committee before the city permit, so the HOA's review runs in parallel with the city's — not after.
3. **Demand written denials.** If the HOA says no, require the denial in writing with a stated justification tied to specific governing-document provisions. "Not in keeping with the community" is not a justification — it's an opinion, and the statute's reasonableness test eats opinions for breakfast.
4. **Keep the city permit moving.** The HOA's review and the city's review are independent tracks. A city-issued permit doesn't moot the HOA process, but a city-approved plan that the HOA rejects puts the burden squarely on the HOA to explain *why* — under a statute that presumes their restriction is the problem.
5. **Document the cost delta.** If a design requirement adds cost, get a contractor's written estimate of the delta. "Unreasonably increases the cost to construct" is in the statute — a dollar figure turns a vague dispute into a concrete one.

## The bottom line

| HOA move | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Outright ADU/JADU ban in a planned development | **Void** — Civil Code 4751 |
| Restriction in a non-CID planned development | **Void** — Civil Code 714.3 |
| ADU prohibition by a condo association | **Allowed** — different regime |
| Reasonable design guidelines (color, style) | **Enforceable** — if cost-neutral-ish |
| Architectural review with stated timelines | **Enforceable** |
| Owner-occupancy requirement on a standard ADU | **Not allowed** |
| Blocking rentals of 30+ days | **Not allowed** |
| Vague "not in keeping" denial, no written justification | **Weakest possible position** for the HOA |

Your HOA can make your ADU look nice. It can't make it not exist. Know the statute number — 4751 — before your first architectural committee meeting.
