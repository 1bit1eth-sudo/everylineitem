Your lot is 50 feet wide. Your city's zoning says accessory structures need 10-foot side setbacks. That would leave you a 30-foot-wide building envelope — and maybe a much smaller ADU than you planned.

State law says otherwise. This guide explains the setback and lot-coverage rules that actually govern your ADU's footprint, and what a forced smaller floor plan means for your costs.

> This is a general guide to California ADU law, not legal advice. Local ordinances vary; confirm your city's specific standards before designing.

## The 4-foot cap

Gov. Code **§66321(b)(3)** sets a ceiling on how far from the property line a city can push your ADU: **no more than 4 feet from the side and rear lot lines**. A city cannot apply its standard R-1 setbacks (10 feet, 20 feet, whatever the zone says) to an ADU.

This is not an obscure reading of the statute — it is how cities implement the law locally. Burbank's ADU handout, for example, states the requirement plainly: **4-foot rear and sideyard setbacks** for ADUs. That is the local implementation of the state cap.

## Conversions: no setback required

If you are converting an existing structure — a garage, a pool house, a section of the home itself — **no setback is required** under state law. The existing walls stand where they stand, even if that means the ADU wall sits directly on or near the property line.

The 4-foot rule governs new construction. Conversions are governed by what already exists.

## Lot coverage, FAR, and open space still apply

Here is where owners get surprised. The setback cap limits how far from the *edges* the city can push you — but local rules about how much of the lot the building can *cover* (lot coverage), how much total floor area is allowed relative to lot size (FAR, floor area ratio), and required open space **still apply** to ADUs.

A typical interaction:

- Your lot is 5,000 sq ft with a 45% lot coverage limit: the building footprint (house + ADU + garage) cannot exceed 2,250 sq ft.
- Your existing house covers 1,800 sq ft. That leaves 450 sq ft of footprint for the ADU — even though the setback rules would happily let you build bigger.
- Or FAR caps total floor area across stories, which can force a single-story design even where a second story would otherwise be allowed.

These rules can shrink the ADU well below what setbacks alone would permit.

## The floor cities must allow

There is a backstop. Under Gov. Code **§66314(d)(7)**, a local agency must allow at least:

- **800 sq ft** of floor area,
- **16 feet** of height,
- with **4-foot** side and rear setbacks,

even if that unit would otherwise violate local coverage, FAR, or open space standards.

So if coverage rules would force you down to a 600 sq ft ADU, the city must still let you build the 800 sq ft minimum. But note what the floor does *not* do: it does not entitle you to the 1,000 or 1,200 sq ft ADU you originally drew. Everything above 800 sq ft is subject to the local rules.

Front setbacks work the same way. Cities can impose front-yard setback standards, but they **cannot use them to block the 800 sq ft minimum**. If applying the front setback would make the minimum unit impossible on your lot, the city must yield to the state floor.

## The cost implication: size changes your fee tier

Setbacks and coverage do not just change your floor plan — they can change your fee math. Two effects matter:

**1. Dropping below the 750 sq ft cliff saves real money.** California law exempts ADUs of 750 sq ft or less from impact fees and school district fees. If coverage rules force you from an 800 sq ft design down to 749 sq ft, you may lose a few square feet of space — and gain thousands of dollars in fee savings. Run both sizes through your city's fee schedule before assuming bigger is better.

**2. Lost square footage is lost rent.** A smaller ADU commands less rent and appraises for less. If the forced-downsize drops you from 800 to 600 sq ft, the fee savings may not compensate for the rent you lose over a decade. Model it both ways.

## Check your city's specific standards

State law sets the frame — 4-foot setback cap, no setback for conversions, the 800 sq ft floor — but every city writes its own implementing ordinance with its own coverage percentages, FAR ratios, and open space definitions. Before you commit to a design:

1. Pull your city's ADU ordinance or handout and find the lot coverage and FAR numbers for your zone.
2. Measure your existing building coverage against the limit — that is your remaining ADU envelope.
3. Confirm the ADU counts (or does not count) toward coverage the way you expect — some cities exempt certain structures.
4. If the math pushes you below 800 sq ft, invoke the state floor; if it pushes you to exactly 749 sq ft, price the fee savings.

The setback cap protects you from being pushed off the edges of your lot. Coverage and FAR decide how much of the middle you can fill. Design for both.
