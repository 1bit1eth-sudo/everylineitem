"Six months, start to finish." It is the most common sentence in ADU sales, and it is missing three to four months of paper.

Nobody lies, exactly. The six months usually describe **construction** — the part with hammers. Before the hammers come the drawings, the submittal, the plan check rounds, the corrections, the resubmittals, and the inspections scheduled between them. This post maps the paper timeline phase by phase, with the statutory clocks California actually guarantees and an honest account of where the clocks stop.

## The statutory clocks

California law gives ADU applicants three enforceable timelines. Know them before you submit anything:

| Clock | What | Law |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **15 business days** | City must tell you whether your application is **complete** — with an exhaustive list of anything missing | Gov. Code §66317 (SB 543, 2026; previously 30 days) |
| **60 days** | City must **approve or deny** a complete application, ministerially — no hearing. Miss it and the application is **"deemed approved"** | Gov. Code §66317 |
| **30 days** | Site-specific review of a **pre-approved ADU plan** (detached, unchanged details) | AB 1332 (Gov. Code §65852.27) |

Two fine-print notes. First, the 60-day clock runs from a **complete** application — the completeness review is its own phase, and every corrections round restarts portions of it. Second, "deemed approved" is real law, but invoking it against your own building department is a relationship decision; most applicants treat the clock as leverage for follow-up, not litigation.

## The realistic timeline

Here is what the phases actually look like, with honest ranges:

| Phase | Typical duration | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design & engineering | 4–12 weeks | Architect/drawings, Title 24 energy, structural. Pre-approved plans cut this sharply but site-specific work remains. |
| Completeness review | 1–3 weeks | City checks the submittal against its checklist. Statutory max: 15 business days. |
| Plan check, round 1 | 3–6 weeks | Structural, fire, planning, public works review in parallel. |
| Corrections | 2–4 weeks | You (or your designer) answer every redline. This is the applicant's clock, not the city's. |
| Plan check, rounds 2–3 | 2–4 weeks each | Each round should be shorter — in practice, new comments appear. |
| Permit issuance | 1–2 weeks | Fee payment, final stamps. |
| **Submittal → permit** | **~3–6 months typical** | The honest range for a custom design. |
| Construction | 5–8 months | The "six months" in the quote — for the build only. |
| Inspections | During construction | Foundation, framing, rough utilities, final. Scheduled 1–2 weeks out in busy jurisdictions. |
| Utility hookup | **Up to 9–12 months** | The forgotten phase — see below. |
| **First drawing → move-in** | **~12–20 months** | The number nobody puts in the quote. |

## Where the time actually goes

**Corrections cycles.** The single biggest variable. A clean first submittal with a responsive designer clears in two rounds; a sloppy one generates four. Every round is 2–4 weeks of city review plus 2–4 weeks of your response time. Ask your designer, in writing, how many rounds their last five ADU submittals took in your city.

**The applicant's own clock.** Designers get busy. Soils reports take 3–6 weeks to schedule. Title 24 consultants queue. Roughly half of "permit delay" stories we hear are applicant-side delay wearing a city costume. Track both clocks separately.

**Pre-approved plans: the honest version.** AB 1332's 30-day site-specific review is real, and cities like Riverside (Dwell Riverside), Salinas, Garden Grove, and Anaheim publish the plan sets. But "site-specific" still means soils, grading, setbacks, utilities, fire access, and foundation — the parts most likely to generate corrections. The 30 days cover the unchanged plan details, not the site. Expect the program to save you 4–8 weeks of design and one plan-check round, not to teleport you to issuance.

**The utility hookup.** Finished ADU, no meter, no power — and waits of 9–12 months documented in legislative testimony. SB 1196 (effective Jan 1, 2027) orders the CPUC to set hard hookup deadlines and lets applicants file the hookup request alongside the building permit. Until then, **start the utility conversation during design, not after final inspection.** It is the longest phase with the least visibility, and it starts the day you first call, not the day you finish building.

## How to protect your schedule

1. **Submit complete.** The 15-day completeness clock only helps if your submittal survives it. Use the city's checklist literally, item by item.
2. **Answer corrections in one pass.** Batch every redline into a single resubmittal. Drip-feeding answers restarts rounds.
3. **Get the designer's round history in writing** before you hire them.
4. **File utilities early.** Parallel-track the hookup request with plan check, not after final inspection.
5. **Calendar the 60 days.** Note the date your application was deemed complete. A polite "day 45" check-in citing the statute moves files.

## Disclaimer

Timeline guide, not legal advice. Statutory clocks are statewide; implementation varies by city. Construction durations depend on scope, site, and market conditions. Always confirm current submittal requirements with your city's building department.
