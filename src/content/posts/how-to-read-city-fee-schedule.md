Every number in this site's city series started as a line in a PDF. The fee schedule — the document cities adopt each year that prices every permit, review, inspection, and fee they offer — is the single most useful public document in ADU economics, and it is one most homeowners never open. Here is how to read one: where they live, the two pricing models cities use, which lines apply to an ADU, and the one declared number that drives everything.

This is a companion to our [permit pricing models](/posts/permit-pricing-models/) explainer. That post describes the models; this one teaches you to read the documents.

## Where fee schedules live

Almost every California city posts its fee schedule on its own .gov site, usually under Community Development, Building & Safety, or Planning, and usually inside a "Document Center" or "Fee Schedule" page. The addresses are rarely memorable — they look like document-database URLs — so search the city name plus "master fee schedule" and confirm the domain ends in the city's official site. A few real examples from cities we have published:

- **Garden Grove** — the full 2026 User Fees PDF: [ggcity.org/sites/default/files/2026-user-fees.pdf](https://ggcity.org/sites/default/files/2026-user-fees.pdf)
- **Torrance** — publishes two separate schedules: a [Building Valuation Rate Table (FY 2026/27)](https://www.torranceca.gov/files/assets/city/v/3/government/community-development/doc-files/building/valuation-tables/valuation-rate-table-20262027.pdf) and a [Planning Fee Schedule](https://www.torranceca.gov/files/assets/city/v/4/government/community-development/doc-files/planning/development-application-amp-supplemental-forms/planning-fee-schedule.pdf)
- **Fullerton** — the FY 2025–2026 Adopted Schedule of Fees & Charges (Table No. 1-A covers building permits): [cityoffullerton.com](https://www.cityoffullerton.com/home/showpublisheddocument/10011/638900771868662816)
- **Downey** — the Proposed City User Fee Schedule FY 2026-2027, Section 6 (Building & Safety): [downeyca.org](https://www.downeyca.org/home/showpublisheddocument/10645/639185148006300000)
- **Santa Maria** — the 2026 User Fee Schedule, 274 fee types, adopted June 2026, effective August 1, 2026: [cityofsantamaria.org](https://www.cityofsantamaria.org/home/showpublisheddocument/26642/639215444670700000)
- **Oxnard** — the Master Fee Schedule, Exhibit A, Section IX (building), linked from the [Building & Engineering page](https://www.oxnard.gov/community-development/building-engineering)
- **Chula Vista** — ADU fees live in the Development Services [fee bulletins](https://chulavistaca.gov/departments/development-services) (Bulletin 10-400 for permits, Bulletin 16-100 for impact fees)

Two habits will save you hours. First, check the fiscal year on the cover: cities adopt a new schedule each year, usually effective July 1, and an old PDF in a search result can quietly be one revision behind. Second, expect the building and planning sections to be separate — in Torrance they are literally different documents, and the ADU impact fee lives in the planning one.

## The two pricing models: valuation tiers vs flat fees

Most cities price the building permit off the project's declared valuation — the estimated construction cost — using tiered brackets. Fullerton's FY 2025–2026 schedule is a clean example of the formula style: at a $250,000 valuation, the permit is $623 for the first $100,000 plus $4.00 per additional $1,000, totaling **$1,223**. Downey's is the same idea with different constants: at an assumed $150,000 valuation, $2,171.42 plus $10.32 per $1,000 over $100,000 gives **$2,687.42** for the permit. Oxnard's FY 2026–27 table prices the $150,001–$750,000 bracket at $1,343.18 plus 0.47% of the amount over $150,000. Torrance's $100,001–$250,000 tier reads $1,471.39 plus 61.12 × $10.65.

The other model is the flat fee: one number, no valuation math. Chula Vista is the only city in our series to price ADU permits this way. Fee Bulletin 10-400 (September 2024) lists a detached ADU on a City Standard Plan at **$3,951** — published as $476 intake + $533 plan check + $2,942 inspection — and a custom detached design at **$4,963**. Flat fees are the easiest to budget from your desk and the hardest to argue with.

Note the trap both models share: the permit is only the cover charge. Every schedule stacks percentage-based reviews on top of it, and the percentages are where the money is.

## The add-ons: plan check and its multipliers

Once you have the permit number, look for the lines expressed as percentages of it. Fullerton is the most instructive example in the series: plan review at **87%** of the permit ($1,064 on that $1,223 permit), energy plan check at **20%** ($245), NPDES at **5%** ($61). The permit-plus-reviews bundle lands near $2,593 — still the series' lowest, but less dramatically than the $1,223 headline. Garden Grove runs plan check at **65%**, with green building and energy add-ons at **+10% each**, cumulative. Torrance prices plan review at **70%** of the permit. Downey at **85%** — its $150,000-valuation example totals roughly $4,972 once the plan check is included.

Then come the small fixed lines: permit issuance fees (Downey: $60.01), technology fees (Downey: 8% of the permit), state SMIP seismic fees (0.013% of valuation on residential), building-standards administration fees (Downey: $6), and Fullerton's General Plan Update Fee at $2 per $1,000 of valuation. None is large; together they are a few hundred dollars you should not forget.

## Which line items actually apply to an ADU

Search the schedule PDF for "ADU" or "accessory dwelling unit" before you trust any line. Four categories matter:

1. **The ADU-specific lines.** The best schedules name ADUs directly. Torrance's Planning Fee Schedule has one line that does double duty as the city's entire 750 sq ft cliff policy: *"Accessory Dwelling Unit: $2,243.47/unit — ADU 750 SF or greater in size."* In every other city we have published, the cliff lives in the ordinance; in Torrance the fee schedule defines it. Chula Vista's Fee Bulletin 16-100 (October 2025) is the other model document: it states that ADUs under 750 sq ft and JADUs are fully exempt from development impact and in-lieu fees, and that larger ADUs pay proportionally to the primary residence.
2. **Impact fees that scale.** Most cities' schedules list park, traffic, drainage, and similar fees per unit or per square foot without ADU math — Garden Grove publishes $6,061 per unit for park mitigation, $1,600 per unit for transportation, and $0.27 per sq ft for drainage. How the city converts those into an ADU number is often unpublished; treat any schedule-to-ADU conversion you do yourself as an assumption until the city confirms it.
3. **Utility capacity charges.** These apply on new connections, not on sharing the primary home's existing connection — which is why most city posts show $0 for water and sewer on a typical ADU. If the schedule lists capacity charges, check the ordinance for the new-connection trigger before budgeting them.
4. **School district fees — usually not in the city schedule at all.** These are levied by school districts under Ed. Code §17620, so they live in district board resolutions and fee-justification studies, not the city PDF. Downey is the exception that proves the rule: its Building Permit Fee Schedule reprints the Downey Unified rate with the disclaimer that fees are subject to change by the district. Always verify the district rate separately.

Anything priced "per dwelling unit" — Torrance lists a $550 Park & Recreation Facilities Tax and a $1,984 Dwelling Unit Tax, plus a 1.5% Construction Tax — needs an ADU-applicability check. Whether a city treats an ADU as a "dwelling unit" for these taxes is exactly the kind of question that requires an email to the city, and we mark such lines as unconfirmed in every city post.

## The declared-valuation trick

Here is the part that matters most. In a valuation-based schedule, the permit fee — and every percentage stacked on it — flows from the declared construction valuation. And in most cities, **the Building Official has the final say on that number**. Downey's schedule says so explicitly: the final valuation is determined by the Building Official.

The standard reference for what the valuation should be is the **ICC Building Valuation Data (BVD)** table, published periodically by the International Code Council, priced per square foot by occupancy and construction type. Torrance's numbers are consistent with the ICC BVD August 2024 figure for R-3 Type VB construction (around $201.40/sq ft) — though the city has not confirmed that it uses it, which is why our Torrance post marks the valuation as assumed.

The trick is that some cities publish shortcuts that undercut the ICC figure. Fullerton's schedule lists "Residential Addition $88.00/sq ft" as a valuation shortcut — at $88/sq ft, an 800 sq ft ADU values at just $70,400, which would cut the permit roughly in half versus a $250,000 ICC-style valuation. Which number the city actually applies to a detached ADU is unconfirmed in Fullerton, and it is the single highest-leverage open question in that post. The lesson generalizes: when a schedule offers both an ICC reference and a shortcut, find out which one your permit counter uses before you budget — the difference can halve or double your permit-plus-reviews total.

And when the schedule fails you, say so. Santa Maria publishes a 274-fee-type User Fee Schedule adopted by council — but the residential building-permit dollar tiers and the plan-check rate could not be read from any public document we found. That post marks them "verify with city" and excludes them from the total rather than estimating. A fee schedule that exists but cannot be read is a signal to visit the counter, not to guess.

## The six-step read

1. Find the current fiscal year's schedule on the city's .gov site (Building & Safety for permits, Planning for impact fees — sometimes separate documents).
2. Locate the building-permit tier table and the city's valuation method (ICC BVD reference, published shortcut, or Building Official discretion).
3. Add every percentage-based review: plan check, energy, green building, NPDES, technology fees.
4. Search the schedule for ADU-specific lines and the 750 sq ft threshold; confirm impact-fee applicability in the ordinance.
5. Verify school district fees and utility capacity charges separately — they usually live outside the city schedule.
6. Flag every "per dwelling unit" tax and every valuation assumption as unverified until the city confirms them.

A fee schedule is a price list, not a quote. Read it as one: the city will still have the last word at the counter, but you will walk in knowing which lines are real, which are assumed, and which question to ask first.

## Disclaimer

Fee-schedule reading guide, not legal or engineering advice. Schedules are readopted annually and local amendments vary; always verify current fees, valuation methods, and ADU applicability with your city before budgeting.
