The most common misunderstanding about AB 1033 is that it gives every California homeowner the right to sell an ADU on its own. It does not. What it does is narrower, and more useful for exactly that reason: it lets your city or county _choose_ to allow separate ADU sales, under state-set conditions. If your city hasn't opted in, the answer is no — no matter what the state law says.

## The state rule: opt-in, not automatic

**AB 1033 (Ting), effective January 1, 2024**, added **Government Code section 66342**. The key word in the statute is "may": a local agency "may also adopt a local ordinance to allow the separate conveyance of the primary dwelling unit and accessory dwelling unit or units as condominiums." A city that never adopts an ordinance has created no separate-sale right, and an ADU in that city stays attached to the primary residence for ownership purposes.

Note what the conveyance is: a **condominium**, not a lot split. The ADU and the primary dwelling become separate condominium units on the same lot, governed by condominium documents — not two lots with two parcel numbers.

## The four requirements every local ordinance must include

Section 66342 says an ordinance "shall include all of the following requirements":

1. **Davis-Stirling compliance.** The condominium must be created under the Davis-Stirling Common Interest Development Act — the state law governing common interest developments, including HOA formation and disclosures.
2. **Subdivision Map Act compliance.** The project must satisfy the Subdivision Map Act and the local subdivision ordinance's objective requirements, including recordation of a condominium plan.
3. **Safety inspection before recordation.** Before the condominium plan is recorded, the ADU must pass a safety inspection — evidenced by a certificate of occupancy or a housing quality standards report from a HUD-certified inspector.
4. **Every lienholder's consent.** Neither a subdivision map nor a condominium plan may be recorded without **each lienholder's consent**, with written evidence and a signed statement provided to the county recorder and recorded with the plan. This is the requirement most often misquoted: the enacted statute requires consent from _each_ lienholder. A pre-enactment committee analysis paraphrased it as "satisfy all liens _or_ get consent" — the statute itself does not offer that alternative.

The statute adds three more conditions: consumer notice about separate conveyance on ADU checklists and permits, notice to utility providers (water, sewer, gas, electricity), and express written authorization from any existing homeowners association.

## What three early adopters actually did

State law is only half the picture. The local ordinance is what you actually apply under, and the three documented adopters differ in instructive ways.

### San Diego County: the newest, and unincorporated-only

On **March 4, 2026**, the County Board of Supervisors adopted **Ordinance No. 10986**, amending Zoning Ordinance Section 6156.x to implement AB 1033. Three limits matter:

- It applies only in **unincorporated** San Diego County. If your ADU is inside an incorporated city, the county ordinance does not cover you — check that city's rules separately.
- The condominium plan is not recorded until **final inspection and a certificate of occupancy**, and HOA documents and CC&Rs are required before tentative map approval.
- **JADUs are expressly excluded**: they "are not eligible for separate sale and must remain part of the primary residence."

### San José: parcel map, checklist first

In **June 2024**, the San José City Council approved **Ordinance No. 31095**, incorporating the state provisions and specifying that permitted ADUs meeting all conditions may be conveyed through a **Parcel Map** issued by the Public Works Department. The city's process starts with its ADU Condominium Checklist — only projects meeting every condition and document in the checklist may proceed to a Parcel Map application — and ends, after recordation, with a new address assignment for the ADU (it can no longer be designated "Unit X").

One sourcing note, stated plainly: the city's published checklist PDF itself could not be opened for this article, so the San José details above rest on the city's ADU Condominium Conversions page, which confirms the ordinance number and the June 2024 approval. Verify the current checklist before relying on it.

### Berkeley: a 10-unit cap and tenant protections

Berkeley's **Ordinance No. 7,999-N.S.** (Municipal Code Chapter 21.29) is the most detailed of the three, and the most protective of sitting tenants:

- **No more than 10 total condominium units per lot**, counting all residential, commercial, and accessory dwelling units.
- **JADUs are ineligible** for conversion to condominiums.
- Tenant protections from Chapter 21.28 apply in full: tenants keep a continued right to occupy after conversion, and hold an **exclusive right to purchase the unit, capped at 90 days** from notice.
- Proof of service of a Notice of Tenants' Rights is required for each tenant household, **no earlier than 60 days before the application date**.

No final adoption date for the Berkeley ordinance is stated here: the council item reviewed was passed to print on January 20, 2026, and the signed ordinance's adoption date was not visible in that document.

## What it costs: items, not amounts

No official fee schedule for AB 1033 conveyances was found, so this article states **no fee amounts** — only the cost items to budget for:

- Parcel map / condominium plan preparation and recordation
- Safety inspection (certificate of occupancy or HUD-certified inspection report)
- Lienholder consent process (lender review, if charged)
- HOA formation documents and CC&Rs
- Possible new or separate utility connections and related connection fees or capacity charges — the Senate Housing analysis notes a local agency, special district, or water corporation may require these upon separate conveyance
- New address assignment (San José's final step)

## The questions to ask before you start

1. Has my city or county adopted an AB 1033 ordinance? (If not, stop here.)
2. Does my lender consent — in writing, before I spend on maps?
3. Is the unit an ADU or a JADU? (JADUs are out.)
4. Does an HOA exist, and will it authorize the conversion in writing?
5. Are there tenants, and what notice and purchase rights does the local ordinance give them?
6. Will a separate utility connection be required, and what does it cost?

## The bottom line

AB 1033 is a permission slip for cities, not a right for homeowners. Where your city has opted in, a separate ADU sale is real but gated: condominium form, four state requirements, every lienholder's consent, and local rules that can add caps and tenant protections. Start with your city's ordinance — and with your lender — before you start with a map.
